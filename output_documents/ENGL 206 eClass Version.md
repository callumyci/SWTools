## Statement on ChatGPT, Bing, and other Generative AIs

  preparation, but ChatGPT does not constitute a valid source for
  citation and quotation
- Factual inaccuracy, cultural bias, unreviewed source, no data beyond
  2021

  anotherʼs substantial editorial or compositional assistance on an
  assignment as the Studentʼs own work.' Submitting work created by
  generative AI and not indicating such would constitute cheating as
  defined above.
- The value and purpose of developing your own voice is essential to
  establishing creative, critical, analytic, research, and communication
  skills: for example, in our class we practice 'writing to learn'
  because that's how you develop those skills and retain knowledge: if
  you're getting a machine to do it for you, save your money and quit
  university because you're not learning.

##Week 1: Introductions & Syllabus Review (Jan 05)

Course Description: This course will provide an introduction to a
diverse range of poets and poetries with a focus on poetic forms, modes
of production and circulation, and contexts of reception in order to
think about ways poetry produces meaning and value. Weʼll read some
conventional and canonical poetry alongside innovative and experimental
works, examples of poetry in popular culture and various media, and
weʼll do so with an attention to the work of creative individuals across
diverse cultures, histories, and geographies. Weʼll develop some
terminology for talking about poetry, and weʼll explore strategies for
thinking about poetic meaning and where, and how, it might be located.

##Week 3: Figurative Language Part One (Jan 17-19)

We'll spend a couple of weeks talking about figurative language in
poetry, focusing on rhetorical tropes such as metaphor, conceit, simile,
metonymy, synecdoche, apostrophe, and personification. We'll also spend
some time developing our close reading skills and refining strategies
for engaging poetry at the level of literal and figurative meaning.

Shelley 'Ozymandias'; Yeats 'The Second Coming'; Rich "Diving into the
Wreck"; Frost 'The Road not Taken'; Hughes "Harlem"; Atwood "\[you fit

##Week 9: Case Study: Eliot's The Waste Land (Feb 28-Mar 02)

Read T.S. Eliot's The Waste Land for this week! We'll likely focus on
the first two sections, "The Burial of the Dead" and "A Game of Chess,"
on Tuesday. So try to have those read, at least, by then.

Perhaps one of the most celebrated poems in literary history, this is a
very challenging read. If you go to the bottom of the poem, you'll find
this version includes some helpful footnotes and, in bold type,
occassionally Eliot's own footnotes to the poem.

But try not to get bogged down in the details with this one: instead,
follow the play of metaphor, imagery, tone, and voice.

##Week 14: Case Study: A Poetics of Place (April 04-06)

For our last full week of classes, we'll be reading and talking about
Matthew James Weigel's 2022 volume of poetry Whitemud Walking . Weigel
is a Dene/Métis poet and artist based here in Edmonton. His book is
available for purchase at the University Bookstore in either print or
digital versions. I'd strongly recommend the print version--it's a
beautiful book! You can also purchase Whitemud Walking at a local
independent bookstore, such as Audrey's Books downtown, or Glass
Bookshop on 76th Avenue. This will be a chance for us to bring together
many of the discussions we've had during the term and focus on a
brilliant piece of work. Please purchase and read the book in advance of
Tuesday's class. Matthew will be joining us for a reading and
discussion!
